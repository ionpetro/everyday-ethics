# Everyday Ethics: foundations authoring and review

Authored and checked 10 November 2026. Scope: units 01–06, the first half of the Everyday Ethics course. This is original educational content adapted to the existing Agora family engine, not a record of anyone's private experience or an assessment of moral character.

## Delivered curriculum

Six units, four lessons each, six native semantic items per lesson: **24 lessons and 144 items**. Every lesson contains a principle with two distinct inline checks, a concept card, an applied fictional case, a sorting or matching distinction, a misconception repair, and a separately held-back transfer case. The 24 transfers each link to the principle earlier in the same lesson. There are 48 four-option scenario items with an explanation for every option; keyed positions vary.

| Unit | Four lessons | Intended capability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| u01 honesty — Honest, without being cruel | Your friend's big idea; A true sentence can mislead; Correct the claim, not the character; Honesty is not total disclosure | Support people without unsupported reassurance; address material omissions; correct claims without inventing intent; use truthful privacy boundaries. |
| u02 facts — What do we actually know? | The story and the evidence; Say how sure you are; Good intentions, real effects; Changing your mind has a reason | Separate observations and inferences, calibrate confidence, distinguish intention from effect, and revise only what new evidence actually changes. |
| u03 perspectives — More than one way to look | Outcomes and boundaries; Character and relationships; Fair shares and shared conditions; When the perspectives disagree | Distinguish ethical perspectives, identify the concern each emphasizes, and explain a choice without treating labels as votes or a mechanical verdict. |
| u04 care — Care that pays attention | Hear the actual request; Help without taking over; A kind no can be clear; Care has more than one person in it | Match help to the actual request, preserve agency, state feasible boundaries, and consider competing dependencies and capacity. |
| u05 consent — A real chance to choose | A yes to what, exactly?; A choice people can understand; When someone changes their mind; Could they really say no? | Specify permission scope, explain important conditions, distinguish controllable future use from irreversible past exposure, and examine real pressure rather than relying on an optional label. |
| u06 fairness — Fair by which rule? | Equal is one possible rule; Relevant merit, visible standards; Fairness in the process; Scarcity does not choose the rule | Apply explicit need, task-relevance, process, and equal-chance criteria while recognizing that a shortage does not select the ethical rule by itself. |

## Sources and epistemic limits

Read the [Markkula framework](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/a-framework-for-ethical-decision-making/) (E1) and its [introduction and limits](https://www.scu.edu/ethics/ethics-resources/ethical-decision-making/introduction-to-a-framework-for-ethical-decision-making/) (E2). They support examining a situation from multiple ethical perspectives while keeping judgment with the person. Neither promises consensus, supplies a universal algorithm, nor validates this app's learning outcomes.

Every card identifies its provenance as ethics, practical synthesis, or fiction. The fictional numbers, people, options, short teaching routines, and memory examples were authored for this course. They are not research findings and do not claim that the linked sources tested those scenarios. No empirical effect sizes, behavior-change guarantees, legal conclusions, or claims of professional qualification are introduced. No private meeting, legal consultation, or user record was used.

Ethical principles are explicitly marked normative. Keyed questions ask about stated facts, scope, evidence, a disclosed decision standard, or which answer actually performs the task in the question. They do not ask which person is good or which contested value is universally superior. Unit 03 explicitly preserves reasoned disagreement; unit 06 makes the chosen allocation criterion visible. Fieldwork uses invented situations rather than asking learners to disclose private conflicts or confront anyone.

## Quality and safety choices

- Every unit has substantive boundary content, including honest nondisclosure, new evidence with unresolved questions, disagreement among perspectives, limits on care, respected refusals despite unequal status, and differing allocation rules under scarcity.
- All moral judgment and scenario practice is untimed. Six neutral practical terms explicitly allow Quick recall: Material omission, Attribution, Inference, Calibrated confidence, Impact, and Revisability. The speed task recalls a definition, not a decision about someone's worth.
- Cases state decision-relevant conditions such as explicit permission limits, verified evidence, feasible alternatives, no emergency, or an agreed rule. A helpful intent does not supply missing permission. A good result does not prove a motive. A nominally private route is not universally assumed safe.
- No fieldwork requires contacting another person, making a financial commitment, revealing personal information, or carrying out an unsafe confrontation.

## Checks and actual review coverage

The six unit files passed the existing course validator with **0 errors**. There are **24 warnings** for deliberately compact distinction sets: sorting uses four explained examples and matching uses three distinct pairs. These meet the supported schema minimums. The shorter sets keep each exercise focused on the lesson's distinction; they are not missing fields or failing generated questions. The production course excludes the example fixture.

Author review read every principle, inline check, term, scenario option and explanation, sorting row, matching pair, misconception repair, boundary, transfer, and fieldwork instruction in these six units. Transfer prerequisites and source IDs were checked structurally. Final integration and browser coverage are recorded by the site owner separately; author validation is not a claim of browser or learning-efficacy testing.

An independent reviewer read all six units and reported two specific issues, both corrected:

1. `u02-l4-misconception`: absence alone did not disprove an accusation. The revised case specifies an allegation of taking a box in person at noon, verified footage showing another person, and the accused teammate elsewhere.
2. `u02-l2-contrast`: the guarantee definition could also fit a broad prediction. It now means an assurance that a specified outcome will occur.

The owner and independent reviewer also flagged overly easy distractors. **52 scenario distractors in units 01–04 were rewritten** into plausible beginner mistakes: delaying a known correction to protect feelings, making an unauthorized helpful decision, treating notice afterward as permission, substituting an unverified workaround, and overextending a valid inference. The first budget case now contrasts surfacing the cost with postponing it, canceling on the friend's behalf, or offering unsupported reassurance. Correct-option lengths and positions were considered during this pass. Some introductory inline checks remain deliberately easy contrasts; a structural pass does not establish optimal difficulty.
